Fabric Science

The Ultimate Guide to Caring for Cashmere, Silk, and Fine Wool

Dr. Elena Rostova Head of Textile Chemistry • Published Aug 24, 2026

Natural animal fibers like cashmere from Himalayan goats and mulberry silk filaments are organic protein polymers. Unlike synthetic polyester or sturdy cotton denim, they are dynamic materials that absorb moisture, react to ambient heat, and require delicate nourishment to stay resilient.

1. Why Heat & Friction Are the True Enemies

When animal hair fibers (wool, alpaca, cashmere) encounter hot water, the microscopic scales on each fiber open up. If agitation or friction occurs while the scales are open, they lock together permanently—this is what causes your favorite $300 sweater to shrink into a child-sized felt block.

Master Cleaner Tip: Never wash fine knitwear in water above 25°C (77°F). Always use a dedicated wool detergent that contains natural lanolin to replenish the fiber's protective oils.

2. The Towel Roll Drying Method (Never Wring!)

Wet wool fibers lose up to 30% of their tensile strength. If you hang a wet wool or cashmere garment from a hanger, the weight of the water stretches the shoulders out of shape permanently.

  1. Step 1: Gently press the garment against the basin to express excess water without twisting.
  2. Step 2: Lay the garment flat on a clean dry white towel.
  3. Step 3: Roll the towel up like a sushi roll with the sweater inside.
  4. Step 4: Gently press down on the roll so the towel absorbs 80% of the dampness.
  5. Step 5: Unroll and lay the garment flat on a drying rack away from direct radiators or sunlight.
"A high-quality 2-ply Scottish cashmere sweater can easily last 25 to 30 years if properly nourished with steam and stored with red cedar blocks rather than plastic dry-cleaning bags."
— Marcus Dupont, Master Tailor & Garment Inspector

3. De-Pilling Like a Professional

Pilling is a natural occurrence caused by loose surface fibers rubbing together (under the arms, at the waist where bags brush). Never pull pills with your bare fingers, as this pulls deeper fibers out of the yarn core and accelerates future pilling. Instead, use a motorized battery de-piller or a fine-mesh cashmere comb held at a 45-degree angle.

4. Seasonal Storage: Moth Protection

Clothes moths do not eat synthetic fibers—they hunt for keratin protein, human skin flakes, and body sweat. Before packing your winter coats away for summer, always have them professionally wet-cleaned. Store them in breathable cotton zip bags with aromatic red cedar blocks or lavender sachets.
